
# Genesis 1:29–31: The Culmination of the Six-Day Creation—Provision for Creatures, Comprehensive Blessing, and the Ultimate Verdict of Being "Very Good"

**New American Standard Bible (NASB):**

> 1:29 Then God said, “Behold, I have given you every plant yielding seed that is on the surface of all the earth, and every tree which has fruit yielding seed; it shall be food for you; 
>
> 1:30 and to every animal of the earth and to every bird of the sky and to everything that moves on the earth which has life, I have given every green plant for food”; and it was so. 
>
> 1:31 And God saw all that He had made, and behold, it was very good. And there was evening and there was morning, the sixth day.

## I. The Placement of This Passage within the Literary and Theological Structure of Chapter 1

This is the concluding passage of the six-day creation narrative—a summary verdict for the entire account. It builds upon the covenantal commission in verses 1:26–28, where humanity is created and entrusted with the mandate to exercise dominion, thereby completing a threefold cycle:

1. **The Cycle of Provision:** Immediately following the delegation of dominion, God establishes an order for sustenance. By defining the original dietary boundaries within the Garden of Eden, God incorporates the allocation of created resources into the environmental terms of the creation covenant. God first bestows the status and mission of a steward, and subsequently provides the means to sustain life; the order of grace is: identity first, followed by provision.

2. **The Cycle of Structure:** The symmetrical "forming and filling" structure of the six days reaches its culmination. The first three days establish the realms, while the subsequent three days populate them with created beings. By verse 1:31, all realms, all levels of creation, and all the necessary order for life are fully in place. God then issues a singular, comprehensive verdict covering the entirety of creation: "very good." Whereas the assessments on the preceding days—each simply "good"—pertained to the specific elements created on those individual days, the "very good" here serves as an evaluation of the entire creative enterprise in its totality.

3. **Narrative Transition and Closure:** This passage concludes the sixth day, setting the stage for the narrative of rest in the second chapter (2:1–3). Having completed all His creative work on the sixth day, God’s rest on the seventh day was not a matter of Him being weary and needing to recover; rather, it was an entry into His rest within a world that had been brought to a state of perfect order. This rest also serves as the ultimate promise of the covenant of creation to humanity: should humans remain obedient, they would be able to enter into God’s rest.

Furthermore, this passage is a crucial text for distinguishing between the created order prior to the Fall and the redemptive order that followed it. The dietary provisions described here belong to the original, uncursed order and cannot be directly transposed into the era following the Fall and the Flood.

## II. In-depth, Verse-by-Verse Exegesis

### 1. "Then God said, 'Behold, I have given you every plant yielding seed that is on the surface of all the earth, and every tree which has fruit yielding seed; it shall be food for you.'"

"Behold" serves as a call to attention within Hebrew narrative, inviting us to take note of a sovereign gift: food is not something the earth produces by mere chance, nor is it something humans secure through their own struggle; rather, it is a gift actively distributed by the Creator.

The food provided to humanity falls into two distinct categories: seed-bearing plants and fruit containing seeds.

There is a fundamental boundary here: within the original order of creation, the food allotted to humanity is confined to plant-based resources; the consumption of animal flesh is not permitted. This does not imply that the lives of animals hold less value; rather, in an order where neither sin nor death has yet entered, there is no paradigm of killing for the sake of food.

The detail regarding "seed-bearing" echoes the narrative of the third day: plants possess an inherent potential for reproduction, ensuring that their provision is sustainable rather than subject to total, one-time depletion. God’s provision carries within it an order of abundance and cyclical renewal—a long-term, gracious arrangement rather than a mere stopgap measure.

Viewed through the lens of the covenant, this constitutes a provision clause within the Covenant of Creation. God bestows the produce of the earth as a grace upon those stewards created in His own image. The prerequisite for humans to enjoy this food is the acknowledgment that all resources belong to the Creator; humans are recipients, not autonomous owners. The very act of partaking in this food serves as a response to the sovereignty of God.

### 2. "And to every beast of the earth and every bird of the air—to everything that creeps upon the earth, in which there is the breath of life—I have given every green plant for food."

God’s provision extends to all *nephesh* (living, breathing creatures), not just to humanity. The sustenance of the entire created order relies upon His decree.

A twofold distinction is evident here:

- **Humans:** Plants and fruits (mature produce, ready for immediate consumption—aligned with the human calling to exercise stewardship and enter into rest).
- **Animals:** Green plants.

This distinction does not imply a hierarchy of value; rather, it reflects an order established by God, tailored to the specific needs of different created beings. The Creator has made careful, prior provision for the survival requirements of every kind of living creature.

This reinforces the recurring motif from Chapter 1—that everything is to exist "according to its kind." God established specific modes of existence and dietary boundaries for each category of life; a divine order permeates every dimension of creation.

A crucial connection: It was only after the Flood—within the context of the Noahic covenant—that God broadened these dietary boundaries to permit the consumption of meat (Genesis 9:3). This shift represents a redemptive arrangement, introduced after sin had entered the world and death had begun to reign over creation; it was not part of the original design of creation.

### 3. "And it was so."

This brief phrase continues the consistent pattern established in Chapter 1: the Word of God carries within itself the power to bring about its own fulfillment. The moment He issues a command regarding provision, the order of sustenance for the created realm is firmly set. No created being can exist independently outside the framework of provision He has established.

### 4. "God saw all that He had made, and it was very good."

This marks the theological pinnacle of this passage and serves as the key phrase for the entirety of Genesis 1: "very good" (*tob me’od*).

The assessments given on the preceding days—simply "good" (*tob*)—referred to the specific creations of those individual days. Here, the addition of the adverb *me’od*—meaning "very" or "exceedingly"—serves as an overarching, comprehensive evaluation of the whole.

To clarify the meaning of "very good":

1. It does not imply that every individual created thing possesses infinite, divine perfection. Rather, it means that everything has fulfilled the purpose intended by God; the order is complete, functions are well-suited, boundaries are clearly defined, and everything aligns perfectly with His plan.

2. This "very good" world is an excellent order that allows for the possibility of testing; it is not a rigid, locked-in environment devoid of choice or the opportunity to be put to the test. "Very good" does not mean "indestructible"; it means that the created order is perfect in relation to the purpose for which it was made. God established a wonderful created order characterized by free choice, rather than a mechanical world devoid of moral agency.

This point is foundational to understanding the Fall described in Chapter 3: Sin is not an inherent component built into God’s creation. Instead, sin occurs when a created steward—within this "very good" order—actively crosses the boundaries established by God, thereby causing a rupture in that order.

### 5. “And there was evening, and there was morning—the sixth day.”

This marker for the final day of the six-day narrative maintains the consistent pattern of Hebrew timekeeping, in which a day begins at dusk.

Literally, the sequence of the six days concludes here, setting the stage for the narrative of rest in 2:1–3: the six days represent God’s work of creation, while the seventh day is for rest. The “work” here does not imply toil or labor on God’s part; rather, it refers to His sovereign actions in establishing order, providing sustenance, and entrusting a mission. His “rest” signifies His sovereign enjoyment of a perfectly ordered creation, while also serving as a model of rest for humanity.

## III. Synthesis: Connecting Motifs in Genesis 1–6

### 1. The Culmination of the Motif of Boundaries

From the separation of light and darkness (1:4), the waters above and below (1:6), and the seas and dry land (1:9), we arrive at the establishment of boundaries regarding food in this passage. God’s order is, in essence, a series of clear, defined boundaries. The very nature of sin lies in the rejection of these divinely established limits: in chapter 3, humanity’s desire to eat from the tree forbidden by God constitutes an act of rebellion precisely at the boundary of the established order for food. The setting of limits regarding what may be eaten serves, in itself, as a foundational element for the test within the covenant of creation.

### 2. The Unity of Provision, Stewardship, and Entrustment

God bestows resources upon humanity not for reckless squandering, but in connection with the mandate to "rule" and "subdue" as described in Genesis 1:26–28. The calling to receive provision and the calling to exercise stewardship are two sides of the same coin: while humans receive the grace of the earth’s bounty, they are simultaneously tasked with acting as stewards to safeguard the order that produces such abundance. Enjoying this grace does not equate to having an unrestricted right to dispose of it however one pleases.

### 3. The Timeline of Death and the Curse

Within the original order—characterized as "very good"—death did not exist as a standard mode of existence. Animals fed on green plants, and humans subsisted on fruits and vegetables; there was no killing for the sake of food. Death was not part of God’s original creative design; rather, it entered the created order as a consequence of the curse that followed the arrival of sin (Romans 8:20–22). In Romans 8, the groaning of creation as it awaits redemption reflects a longing to return to this original, "very good" order—and to advance toward the even greater perfection of the new creation.

### 4. The Typological Thread of Sabbath Rest

After completing all His work on the sixth day, God rested on the seventh. This pattern later became the foundation for the Sabbath in Israel (Exodus 20:8–11). The theological basis for Sabbath rest lies here: God rested upon a creation that had already attained the state of being "very good." Humans were called to enter into His rest, contingent upon their continued obedience. Adam’s failure represented a collapse along the path that should have led toward this rest; consequently, the redemptive narrative of the entire Bible is the story of recovering that lost rest (Hebrews 4).

### 5. The Ultimate Counterpart to the New Creation

The "new heaven and new earth" described in the Book of Revelation represent the fulfillment of a new "very good" state. In this new creation, death, suffering, and bloodshed are no more; it marks a return to God’s perfect order—corresponding to the original goodness found in Genesis 1—while simultaneously transcending the initial created order through Christ. The "very good" state described in Genesis 1 serves as both a model and a foreshadowing of the ultimate goal of redemption.

## IV. Clarifying Common Exegetical Misconceptions

### Pitfall 1: "The fact that plants are permitted as food here implies that animals were always intended for human consumption—Adam and Eve simply chose not to eat them at that time."

**Clarification:** The text explicitly distinguishes between two separate provisions for food: green plants are assigned to animals, while fruits and vegetables are assigned to humans. There is no inherent provision within the text suggesting that the consumption of meat was permitted at a later stage. The permission to eat meat—granted in Genesis 9:3, after sin had already entered the world and death had begun to reign—was a concession added by God within the Noahic covenant; it was not part of the original intent of creation.

### Pitfall 2: "‘Very good’ implies that the world held no possibility for sin; therefore, the Fall was an accident outside of God’s plan."

**Clarification:** The term "very good" refers to the created order itself being good; it does not mean that created beings lacked the capacity for moral choice. God’s decision to create beings with the ability to make moral choices was, in itself, good. When humans chose to rebel, it represented a deviation of the created will—not a flaw in God’s creation. That God, in His sovereignty, foresaw and permitted the Fall—while simultaneously preparing for redemption—does not imply that His creation was defective.

### Pitfall 3: "The phrase ‘and it was so’ is merely a formulaic expression with no theological significance."

**Clarification:** Each instance of "God said... and it was so" serves to declare the sovereign efficacy of God’s word. This applies to the provision of food as well: the order governing the distribution of life-sustaining resources did not emerge through the natural processes of evolutionary competition, but was established by His command.

### Pitfall 4: "Plants lack life; therefore, consuming them does not constitute death—which is why God permitted it."

**Clarification:** As previously noted, the Bible distinguishes between *nephesh* (living souls possessing breath and sentience) and the vegetative life found in plants; this does not mean that plants are devoid of life. The focus of this biblical distinction is not on the question of "death" per se, but rather on the fact that the original created order did not include a pattern in which sentient, living souls were killed to serve as food.

## V. Morning Meditation (This Morning with God)

Verses 1:29–31 remind us that the source of all provisions sustaining life is God’s gift. It is easy for us to take food and resources for granted, viewing them merely as the fruits of our own labor. Yet, the creation narrative reveals that the earth’s capacity to yield produce and the ability of seeds to multiply are rooted in His command. Everything we enjoy is, at its core, an act of receiving grace—never something to which we are inherently entitled.

At the same time, the declaration that creation was "very good" reminds us that everything God made originally existed within a beautiful order. The suffering, death, and conflict we witness today are not how the world was meant to be; rather, they are distortions brought about by sin. This realization sustains our hope: through Christ’s redemption, all that has been damaged will be restored, leading creation back to the perfection God intended.

**Reflective Questions:** When I receive my daily provisions, do I remember that they all come from the grace of the Creator? Am I waiting for God to bring the created order back into that state of being "very good"?
