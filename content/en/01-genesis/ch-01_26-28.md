
# Genesis 1:26–28 – The Core of the Sixth Day: The Creation of Humanity in God’s Image and the Commissioning of the Creation Covenant

**New American Standard Bible (NASB):**

> 1:26 Then God said, “Let Us make mankind in Our image, according to Our likeness; and let them rule over the fish of the sea and over the birds of the sky and over the livestock and over all the earth, and over every crawling thing that crawls on the earth.” 
>
> 1:27 So God created man in His own image, in the image of God He created him; male and female He created them. 
>
> 1:28 God blessed them; and God said to them, “Be fruitful and multiply, and fill the earth, and subdue it; and rule over the fish of the sea and over the birds of the sky and over every living thing that moves on the earth.” 

## I. The Placement of This Passage within the Literary Structure of Chapter 1

By this point, the stage for the six days of creation—along with all non-human created beings—has been fully set (verses 1–25). The narrative thus reaches the literary and theological climax of the entire first chapter of Genesis.

### 1. The Theology of Order

The sequence begins with time, the firmament, vegetation, the sun and moon, the living creatures of the sea and air, and the beasts and livestock of the earth; humanity appears last. Humans are the pinnacle and stewards of the creative enterprise, not its starting point. The world is not a randomly generated backdrop for human existence, but a realm meticulously prepared by the Creator and entrusted to humanity for stewardship.

### 2. Grammatical and Narrative Markers

While the commands for all preceding acts of creation follow the pattern "God said, 'Let there be...'"—each referring to a specific category of created things—the account of human creation begins differently: "God said, 'Let Us make man in Our image, according to Our likeness.'" The use of a deliberative, plural form distinguishes this from all other creative commands, highlighting the unique nature of human creation. It originates from the archetype of the Divine Being Himself, rather than emerging from within the existing categories of earthly living creatures.

### 3. A Progression from the Blessing of Living Creatures on the Fifth Day

The blessing bestowed upon living creatures on the fifth day was limited to "be fruitful and multiply, and fill [the earth/spaces]." In contrast, the blessing given to humanity adds the authority to "rule" and "govern" (*radah*). This mission belongs exclusively to those who bear the image of God and constitutes the core responsibility of the Covenant of Creation.

Within the framework of Covenant Theology, this passage marks the pivotal moment in the establishment of the Covenant of Works. Through this covenant, God entrusts the stewardship of the entire earth to humanity—beings created in His own image—who, as covenantal stewards, exercise dominion over the created order on God’s behalf.

## II. In-depth, Verse-by-Verse Exegesis

### 1. "Then God said, 'Let Us make mankind in Our image, in Our likeness.'"

#### ① The Plural "Us"

Classic interpretations of this plural form in the Old Testament include:

- From a Christian Trinitarian perspective: This represents an internal deliberation among the multiple persons within the Divine Being, serving as an Old Testament foreshadowing of the New Testament doctrine of the Trinity.
- From a grammatical standpoint, it is sometimes explained as a "plural of majesty" (or "royal plural"); however, when viewed in light of the progressive revelation found throughout the entire Bible, it cannot be reduced merely to a royal rhetorical device. To interpret it solely as a "plural of majesty" would diminish the ontological weight—the profound reality—of the "image" as originating from the Divine Being itself.

A crucial distinction exists between "image" (*ṣelem*) and "likeness" (*demuth*):

- **Image (Likeness/Representation):** Emphasizes representation and the reflection of an original archetype. It signifies that human beings possess functional dimensions that mirror God, serving as the foundational basis for humanity’s identity as God’s representatives on earth.
- **Likeness (Resemblance):** Emphasizes similarity and the possession of traits that correspond to the original archetype. It points to an inner resemblance in terms of spirituality, morality, rationality, and the capacity for relationship.

These two concepts are not entirely separate sets of attributes; rather, they are two sides of the same coin. In their creation, human beings are, by identity, representatives of God, and in their characteristics, they possess a created resemblance that corresponds to the Creator.

**⚠️ Key Point: The "image of God" does not refer to the physical appearance of the human body.**

Since God is not a material being, the "image" cannot be understood as physical features. The image of God encompasses rationality, moral conscience, the capacity for relationship (communion with God and with others), and the status of being entrusted with a mandate to govern. Even after the Fall, the image of God has not been entirely lost—James 3:9 refers to human beings as having been made in the likeness of God—though it has been distorted and damaged, requiring renewal in Christ (Colossians 3:10).

#### ② The Use of the Term *Bara’* for the Creation of Humanity

Note the shift in verbs: Throughout the ordering work of the first six days, the term *asah* (to make, set in place, or fashion from existing created material) is used extensively. However, in verse 27—"So God created (*bara’*) mankind in his own image"—the verb *bara’* is employed. This is the same unique verb used in verse 1:1 for the "creation" of the heavens and the earth, signifying the bringing into existence of something entirely new. This serves as a profound theological marker: the dimension of human existence occupies a different level than that of other living creatures on earth—which were formed from the earth or the waters. It represents an act of entirely new creation, which underscores the unique significance of being made "in the image of God."

### 2. "Let them have dominion over the fish of the sea, the birds of the air, the livestock, all the wild animals of the earth, and all the creatures that move along the ground."

The Hebrew verb *radah* (to rule, govern, or exercise dominion) is the key term for authority in this passage.

**✅ Note: The foundation of this *radah* (dominion) is not human ability, but rather the identity of being "created in the image of God."**

Humanity is able to exercise dominion precisely because humans serve as God’s representatives on earth. In essence, this governance is a form of delegated rule—not an autonomous ownership or a license for arbitrary control. Humans are not the masters of the earth; they are God’s stewards.

The scope of this dominion encompasses all the previously created realms: the sea, the air, the livestock on the earth, the entire earth itself, and the creeping things—in short, the whole of the created order as brought into existence by God. Every domain filled during the preceding days is now entrusted to this steward who bears God’s image.

Covenantal Perspective: This mandate to exercise dominion is a responsibility conferred upon humanity by the Covenant of Creation. Within the boundaries of the order established by God, humans are tasked with maintaining, guarding, and tending to His good creation, acting as representatives who exercise God’s own sovereignty over the created world.

### 3. "So God created mankind in his own image, in the image of God he created them; male and female he created them."

Narrative Recapitulation and Narrowing of Focus: The text shifts from "our image" to "His image," immediately introducing the dimension of gender: the creation of male and female.

A crucial truth is presented here: the image of God resides in both men and women; it is not the exclusive possession of the male. Both genders, together, fully embody this created identity. Their complementary relationship serves as a manifestation of the image of God at the relational level (a point that lays the foundation for the narrative of the marital union between the two in the following chapter).

Theological Point: While an individual human being possesses the image of God, the fact that He created them "male and female" reveals that existence in the image of God is, from the very beginning, not that of an isolated individual, but of a relational being—reflecting the relational nature inherent within God Himself (the fellowship of the Trinity).

### 4. "Then God blessed them, and God said to them, 'Be fruitful and multiply; fill the earth and subdue it; have dominion over the fish of the sea, over the birds of the air, and over every living thing that moves on the earth.'"

A twofold blessing and command are layered here:

1. To be fruitful, multiply, and fill the earth: This continues the pattern of blessing given to living creatures on the fifth day, yet the scope is expanded to "filling the entire earth." Procreation and filling the earth constitute a foundational blessing that God bestows upon created life.

2. To subdue the earth and exercise dominion over all living things: This reaffirms the *radah* (dominion/ruling) authority—a restatement of the mission. In Hebrew narrative, such repetition serves to underscore the gravity of the covenantal mandate.

In contrast to the living creatures of the fifth day—which received only the blessing of procreation—humanity receives both the blessing of procreation and a covenantal mission to exercise dominion over the entire earth. This is the unique calling that stems from one’s identity as being made in the image of God.

## III. Synthesis: Connecting Core Motifs in Genesis 1–6

### 1. The Climax of the Motif of Boundaries

All living creatures are created "according to their kinds," confined within the boundaries of their respective created categories. Humans, however, are uniquely fashioned after the archetype of God Himself and are granted authority to exercise dominion over other created kinds. Yet, there remains an impassable boundary for humanity: humans are creatures, not the Creator. The essence of the Fall in Genesis 3 lies in humanity’s attempt to transgress this boundary—to become "like God" (Gen 3:5), to usurp the position of the Archetype, and thereby shatter the covenant of creation.

### 2. The Foundational Basis for the Promise of the "Seed"

The command to "be fruitful and multiply" is a blessing rooted in creation. Following the Fall, this blessing of procreation was not entirely withdrawn; instead, the theme of the "seed" (offspring) shifted toward a promise of redemption—the "seed of the woman" mentioned in Genesis 3:15. The original creational mandate to "fill the earth" was transformed, within the history of the Fall, into a hope for the coming of a redemptive "seed"—a new humanity. From the initial blessing of creation to the subsequent promise of redemption, the thread of the "seed" runs consistently throughout the entire book of Genesis.

### 3. The Emergence of the Complete Framework of the Creation Covenant

- One party to the covenant: God (the Creator and the true Owner of the earth).
- The other party to the covenant: Humanity, created in the image of God (stewards).
- The blessings of the covenant: To be fruitful and multiply, to live securely within a well-ordered creation, and to enjoy the abundance of what has been created.
- The responsibilities of the covenant: To exercise dominion over and care for the earth, and to remain obedient to the Creator’s commands (the prohibition regarding the tree of the knowledge of good and evil in Genesis 2 serves as the probationary clause of this creation covenant).

The logic of the covenant of creation is as follows: in 1:26–28, a mission is entrusted, and in 2:16–17, a command is established as a test of obedience. If humanity were to respect the boundaries and remain in complete obedience, they would enter into a state of perfect rest; conversely, disobedience would bring about death and a curse.

### 4. The Link Between the Fate of Creation and Humanity as the Image-Bearer

The state of the entire created order is bound to the state of humanity, who serve as its stewards. When humans uphold their covenantal mission, the earth continues to flourish within the beautiful order of creation; when they rebel and overstep their bounds, the whole of creation falls together into the bondage of futility (Romans 8:20). This is the underlying logic for the curse upon the earth described in the following chapter (Genesis 3). The destiny of the created order is entrusted to humanity—the stewards who bear the image of God.

### 5. The Thematic Thread of Christ as the Perfect Image-Bearer

Adam, the "first man," possessed the image of God but failed during his time of testing. Christ, the "last Adam" (1 Corinthians 15:45) and the perfect image of God (Colossians 1:15), alone fully lived out the obedience and mission inherent to that image. By fulfilling the covenantal responsibilities that the first Adam failed to keep, He brought about a new creation. The narrative of humanity as the image of God in the first chapter thus lays the groundwork for the redemptive contrast between the first Adam and the last.

## IV. Clarifying Common Exegetical Misconceptions

### Pitfall 1: "The image of God is equivalent to human reason or wisdom; therefore, those who lack such faculties have lost the image of God."

Clarification: The image of God refers to one’s ontological status as a created being, not to acquired capacities. Even individuals with impaired reason or limited mental faculties remain created in the image of God. While the image in fallen humanity is damaged and distorted, it has not been entirely lost. Equating the image of God with specific mental abilities erroneously undermines the foundational dignity of certain human beings.

### Pitfall 2: "The term *radah* (to rule/govern) implies that humans may exploit and plunder the earth without any limitations."

Clarification: The archetype for this "ruling" is God’s own governance of the world—characterized by order, stewardship, and the grace of provision. To rule as a representative (*delegated dominion*) means to look after God’s earth on His behalf; it is not an unrestrained exercise of power. A steward cannot destroy the master’s property in violation of the boundaries established by the master—this is an inherent constraint of the Creation Covenant.

### Pitfall 3: "The creation of humanity as 'male and female' is merely a mechanism for procreation, unrelated to the image of God."

Clarification: The text explicitly places the creation of "male and female" within the same sentence as the creation of humanity "in His own image." Gender relations serve as a significant manifestation of the image of God within the dimension of relationship; the two concepts cannot be severed.

### Pitfall 4: "The 'Creation Covenant' is merely a construct of later theologians; the text itself contains no covenant."

Clarification: Although the specific term *berith* ("covenant") does not appear in Genesis 1, all the structural elements of a covenant are present: a Sovereign bestowing blessings upon a created representative, entrusting them with responsibilities, and establishing boundaries of order. The prohibition found in Genesis 2 functions as a "test clause," completing this covenantal structure—a pattern typical of Hebrew covenantal literature. The Christian theological formulation of the "Creation Covenant" is rooted in the overall structure of the narratives in Genesis 1 and 2; it is not an arbitrary invention.

## V. Perspective for Morning Meditation (This Morning with God)

This passage reveals the source of human dignity: our value does not lie in our abilities, wealth, or status, but in the fact that we are created in the very image of God. This identity precedes any of our subsequent achievements; it is a foundation bestowed by the Creator.

At the same time, it reminds us of our responsibility as stewards: the authority we hold to manage the world and life itself is never an autonomous, sovereign right, but rather a delegated trust. We live in God’s world, and all our stewardship must remain subject to the order and boundaries He has established. The point where the first Adam failed is precisely the area where we continually need to rely on Christ for restoration.

### Questions for Meditation

Do I recognize the dignity of myself and others based on the foundation that we are "created in the image of God"? Do I remember that all the authority I possess in this world is merely that of a steward acting under a divine trust?

## Summary

Genesis 1:26–28 sets forth the core mission of the Creation Covenant: the creation of humanity—male and female—in the image of God, and the calling to be fruitful, multiply, fill the earth, and exercise dominion over it. This marks the theological pinnacle of the creation narrative in the first chapter. Immediately following, in verses 29–31, God further specifies the provision of food for humanity and pronounces the final, comprehensive verdict that all is "very good," bringing the entire six-day work of creation to its conclusion.
