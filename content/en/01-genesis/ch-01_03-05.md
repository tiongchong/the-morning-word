
# Genesis 1:3–5 — Day One: God Says, “Let There Be Light”

**New American Standard Bible (NASB):**

> **1:3** Then God said, “Let there be light”; and there was light.
>
> **1:4** God saw that the light was good; and God separated the light from the darkness.
>
> **1:5** God called the light “day,” and the darkness He called “night.” And there was evening and there was morning, one day.

---

## I. The Position of This Passage within the Overall Literary Structure of Genesis 1

As previously noted, the six-day creation account follows a symmetrical, two-part, three-day structure: the first three days establish realms or spatial orders, while the subsequent three days fill those realms.

- **Day 1:** The establishment of the temporal order of light and darkness, day and night.
- **Day 4:** The creation of the sun, moon, and stars—entities that govern the day and night and serve as signs—thereby completing the filling of the realm established on the first day.

In terms of narrative rhythm, beginning with this passage, the signature repetitive structure of the first chapter makes its full and formal appearance:

```text
God said → it was so → God saw that it was good → established a separation/gave a name → there was evening, and there was morning—the [X]th day.
```

This fixed pattern carries a liturgical quality—reminiscent of a covenantal proclamation—much like a monarch issuing a decree to establish order. In His capacity as King, God uses His word to set the laws for the created order.

It is important to note that Genesis 1:1–2 serves as an overarching introduction and describes the initial state of creation; it is verse 3 that marks the formal commencement of the six-day process of bringing order. In verse 2, the world remains shrouded in “darkness over the deep.” The action of the first day represents God's initial intervention into this darkness—bringing forth light and setting the stage for the establishment of order.

## II. In-depth Exegesis, Verse by Verse

### 1. “And God said, ‘Let there be light,’ and there was light.”

**Core Theological Proposition: Creation is brought into effect by the word of God.**

The “speaking” mentioned here is not mere human communication, but a declarative command possessing creative efficacy. Unlike human speech—which serves primarily to convey information—God's utterance carries the inherent power to bring existence into being. This builds upon the foundation established in verse 1 (“God created”): the same God who brought the heavens and the earth into existence uses His word as the driving force that shapes the created order.

**Biblical-Theological Connections:**

John 1:3 in the New Testament explicitly declares that “all things were made through Him (the Word),” and Hebrews 11:3 states that “the worlds were framed by the word of God.” Both passages point back to this same thread: the creative word—the work of Christ as the Word.

The collaborative work of the Trinity can be understood from a broader biblical-theological perspective: the Father wills and commands, the Word is the agent of creation, and the Holy Spirit, whose presence is described as hovering in Genesis 1:2, is active in the divine work of creation.

> ⚠️ **A Crucial Exegetical Distinction:** The light of the first day ≠ the light from the sun, moon, and stars created on the fourth day.

This is the central exegetical point of this section. Since the sun, moon, and stars were not appointed to their functions until the fourth day, the light mentioned here precedes their establishment; it is not the physical light emitted by celestial bodies as we understand it today.

Its primary significance within the narrative is not simply that of a physical light source, but also as a sign of order—a state of existence characterized by distinction and revelation, standing in opposition to darkness. In the creation motifs of the ancient Near East, darkness and the deep waters were often associated with chaotic forces. By bringing forth light, God declares that darkness does not hold independent dominion and that His divine order takes precedence over chaos.

### 2. “God saw that the light was good.”

**Core Theological Proposition: The goodness of creation is determined by the Creator.**

In the context of the creation narrative, the word “good” (*tov*) does not carry exclusively the moral connotation of “virtuous” or “holy.” Instead, it signifies that something aligns with the Creator's intended purpose and fulfills the order He envisioned; it is a created order that is recognized and approved by God.

This stands in contrast to the *tohu wabohu* (“formless and void”) mentioned in Genesis 1:2. *Tohu wabohu* represents an initial state that is unformed and unfilled. When God's Word intervenes and light appears, it is declared “good”—marking the moment when creation begins to realize the orderly purpose ordained by God.

#### A Covenantal Perspective

The “good” as determined by God refers to the state that creation was intended to occupy within the framework of the creation covenant. The subsequent fall of humanity represents a deviation from the order that God declared “good.”

### 3. “And He Separated the Light from the Darkness”

**Core Theological Proposition: Separation is the fundamental act through which God establishes order.**

“To separate” or “to distinguish” (from the Hebrew root *bādal*) is a core, recurring verb throughout the first chapter of Genesis:

- **Day 1:** Separating light from darkness.
- **Day 2:** Separating the waters above from the waters below, establishing the expanse (firmament).
- **Day 3:** Separating dry land from the seas.

“Separation” is the essential action by which the created order is established. God's work consists of setting clear boundaries, categories, and order within a state that was initially unformed and unfilled.

In the creation myths of various pagan traditions, the central narrative involves deities engaging in battle or conflict against forces of chaos to secure order. Genesis presents a fundamentally different picture: God does not need to wage war against darkness or chaos; He simply issues a command, and the separation is accomplished. From beginning to end, chaos possesses no independent, adversarial sovereignty; it remains entirely under God's control.

### 4. “God Called the Light Day, and the Darkness He Called Night”

**Core Theological Proposition: Naming is a declaration of sovereignty.**

In the royal cultures of the Ancient Near East, the right to name something could signify authority, ownership, and the power to govern.

By naming the day and the night, God declares that both belong to Him and that He is the architect of the temporal order.

#### Foreshadowing

Later, God entrusts humanity with the authority to govern the earth (Genesis 1:26–28), and humans, in turn, name the animals (Genesis 2:19–20). This reflects God delegating stewardship—the authority to govern and to name—to those created in His own image. The act of naming the animals expresses humanity's image-bearing mission and delegated responsibility to exercise dominion.

> ⚠️ **Important Clarification: Darkness Is Not Inherently Evil.**

The Scriptures do not cast a curse upon darkness. God named the darkness “night” as part of the order He established; night itself is not evil. Although darkness later becomes a symbolic image of sin, judgment, and separation from God in various biblical passages, this symbolism should not be projected indiscriminately back into Genesis 1.

On the first day of creation, “night” was simply a component of the temporal order established by God.

### 5. “And There Was Evening, and There Was Morning—the First Day.”

**Core Theological Proposition: Created time operates according to the order established by God.**

The Hebrew narrative sequence here is distinctive: evening precedes morning. This contrasts with the modern Western perspective, where a new day is commonly understood to begin in the morning.

In the traditional Hebrew and Jewish reckoning of time, a day begins at sunset and concludes at the following sunset. The sequence of evening and morning in Genesis 1 is consistent with this pattern.

#### Beyond Mere Ancient Custom: The Narrative Significance of Evening and Morning

A created day begins with evening and moves toward morning. This mirrors the narrative progression within this passage: moving from the darkness over the deep (Genesis 1:2) toward the light brought forth by God.

The very rhythm of created time bears the imprint of a narrative arc: a transition from the initial state of darkness toward the light and order established by God.

#### Regarding the Term “Day” (*yôm*)

Adopting a literal interpretation—a 24-hour cycle of day and night—is, from a straightforward grammatical and literary reading, the most consistent approach when applying the principle of “interpreting Scripture by Scripture” and maintaining fidelity to the original text, especially given how the text's repeated day-and-night formula aligns with subsequent biblical laws and commandments.

#### Core Theological Truths Emphasized by Covenant Theology

- Order is established entirely by the Word of God; it does not emerge spontaneously from chaos.
- Every aspect of the created order is established by God and declared by Him to be “good.”
- The entire created order paves the way for humanity's mission—rooted in the *imago Dei* (image of God)—and the calling to enter into rest.

Scientific debates concerning chronology are secondary issues and should not obscure the central theological narrative presented in this passage.

## III. Synthesis: Connecting This Passage to the Overarching Themes of Genesis 1–6

### 1. The Theme of the Divine Word

This passage presents, for the first time, the complete paradigm of “God speaks, and it comes to pass”—a pattern repeated throughout the subsequent days of creation.

This establishes a foundational principle for the entire book of Genesis: the Word of God is the source of all order, and human life is rooted in obedience to that Word. This theme stands in sharp contrast to Genesis 3, where humanity questions and ultimately rejects God's Word.

### 2. The Theme of Separation and Boundaries

The separation of light from darkness on the first day marks the beginning of all divine acts of “setting boundaries.”

The essence of sin lies in the persistent attempt to erase the boundaries established by God—whether it is humanity in Genesis 3 seeking to overstep its limits to become like God, the blurring of boundaries within the events described in Genesis 6, or the attempt at the Tower of Babel to transcend the boundaries established by God.

### 3. Foreshadowing the Themes of Offspring and Redemption

Within the Old Testament's system of typology, light frequently prefigures the arrival of salvation (e.g., Psalm 27:1; Isaiah 9:2–7). In the New Testament, Christ is identified as the “Light of the world” (John 8:12).

As a light that existed prior to the establishment of the sun and moon, the light of the first day serves as a theological foreshadowing—suggesting that the light of salvation transcends all created sources of light—and ultimately points toward the redemption brought by the “offspring of the woman” mentioned in Genesis 3:15.

### 4. The Long-term Theme of Sabbath Rest

The recurring cycle of day and night provides the fundamental temporal framework for the rhythm of labor and rest, laying the groundwork for the establishment of the seventh-day Sabbath.

## IV. Addressing Common Exegetical Pitfalls

### Pitfall 1: “The Light of the First Day Is Christ Himself.”

**Clarification:**

While one may view this light as a type or symbol of Christ, it is incorrect to equate the light in Genesis 1:3 directly with the person of Christ.

The light described in this passage is part of the created order, whereas Christ is the Creator—not a created being. Types point toward Christ, but they cannot be directly equated with the reality itself; one must avoid confusing the persons or entities involved.

### Pitfall 2: “Darkness Is Satan's Domain; the Light on the First Day Represents a War Against Satan.”

**Analysis:**

This passage belongs to the pre-fallen created order; sin and Satan's rebellion have not yet appeared in the Genesis narrative.

Here, “darkness” simply denotes a created state lacking light—it is not an evil force. Projecting the imagery of darkness associated with the spiritual realm following the Fall back into the first chapter constitutes an *anachronism* in exegesis.

It undermines the foundational premise of the creation narrative: that what God created was “good.”

### Pitfall 3: “Since Light Existed Before the Sun, This Contradicts Science; the Bible Is Merely the Mythological Imagination of Ancient People.”

**Analysis:**

The primary purpose of the text is not to serve as a modern textbook on cosmology, but to make a theological declaration: sovereignty over light sources and the order of time does not reside in the sun or the stars, but in God.

Even in the absence of the sun and moon as appointed rulers of the day and night, God is capable of establishing light, day, and night. Created celestial bodies are simply instruments God uses to fulfill the order He has already established, as seen in their assigned roles on the fourth day.

The theological declaration of the text remains independent of the empirical understanding of celestial bodies held by ancient peoples.

## V. Morning Reflection (Echoing the Perspective from *This Morning with God*)

When we find ourselves in the “darkness over the deep”—experiencing inner chaos, a loss of direction, or the collapse of order in our lives—Genesis 1:3 reminds us that the source of light is never our environment or external circumstances; rather, it is God's Word, which carries creative power.

In the midst of any chaotic situation, He can issue a command that brings forth order and establishes boundaries.

At the same time, this reminds us of our entrusted mission. Since God established “separation” and “order” as blessings within creation, our calling—as those who bear His image—includes the act of discernment: distinguishing between good and evil, between proper boundaries and the transgression of those boundaries, and between the order that aligns with His heart and the chaotic autonomy we might desire for ourselves.

Every temptation to overstep a boundary is, in essence, a rejection of the “separation” established by God—an attempt to erase the limits He has set—much like the temptation posed by the serpent in the Garden of Eden.

### Reflection Questions

1. In what areas of my life am I attempting to erase the boundaries established by God, seeking to overstep the order He has set in place?
2. Do I believe that His Word has the power to bring the light of order back into my life?

## VI. Summary and Preparation for the Next Section of In-depth Study

Genesis 1:3–5 (the first day) marks the formal beginning of the six-day work of creation, characterized by the establishment of order.

Through His Word, God brings light into what was previously a state of darkness and formlessness. He separates light from darkness, names the day and the night, and establishes the temporal order for the entire created realm.

This passage provides the first complete demonstration of the following narrative template:

```text
God speaks → it comes to pass → it is pronounced good → boundaries are set → the day is concluded.
```

This serves as a foundational paradigm for the ordering of the firmament and the waters on the second day.
