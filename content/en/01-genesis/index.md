
# An In-depth, Biblical-Theological Study of Genesis · Introduction

## Foundational Study Framework

Let us first establish the foundational framework for this entire study:
It is centered on **biblical theology**, rather than mere verse-by-verse commentary.

- **The Core of Biblical Theology**: It does not focus on interpreting individual verses in isolation—through historical background or lexical analysis (though these will certainly be addressed)—but rather traces how God’s redemptive plan unfolds progressively throughout the biblical narrative. Key thematic threads include: **Creation — The Fall — Promise — Election — Covenant**.

- **Holistic Perspective**: The Book of Genesis is not merely a collection of disjointed stories about the patriarchs;
  rather, it serves as the prologue and foundation for the entire Bible, as well as the foundational volume for the history of the covenant.

- **The Thread of Covenant Theology**: Within the covenant of grace,
  God chooses a people—amidst a fallen creation—
  to progressively unfold His plan of redemption,
  preparing the way for the promised "Seed"—the Seed of the woman (Genesis 3:15)—
  leading up to the advent of Christ.

## Overview of the Book’s Structure

We will begin by examining the overall structure of the book, followed by an in-depth study of the first section.
Moving forward, we will proceed step-by-step through the subsequent passages of the text.

1. Prologue: The Creation Narrative (Chapters 1–2)
   >God established the created order and the Sabbath,
   entrusting humanity with the mission to steward creation,
   thereby serving as His representatives on earth.
2. Rupture: The Fall and the Curse (Chapters 3–4)
   >Sin entered the world, fracturing relationships—
   between humanity and God, among human beings,
   and between humanity and the created order.
   Two distinct lineages emerged:
   the line of Cain (characterized by worldliness and self-exaltation),
   and the line of Seth (characterized by calling upon the name of the Lord).
3. Judgment and Preservation: The Flood Narrative (Chapters 5–9)
   >The entire earth became corrupt, and judgment ensued.
   Yet, in His grace, God preserved Noah and his family,
   establishing the Noahic Covenant (the covenant of the rainbow)
   to sustain the created order and ensure a remnant for the continuation of redemptive history.
4. The Origin of Nations: The Tower of Babel (Chapters 10–11)
   >Humanity united in rebellion against God,
   only to be scattered following the confusion of languages—an event that gave rise to the nations.
   Humanity cannot reach the heavens through its own efforts;
   it must await God’s sovereign initiative in bestowing grace and choosing His people.
5. The Abraham Cycle (Chapters 12–25)
   > God formally initiates the history of election and covenant, centered on Abraham.
   The core of the promise encompasses land, descendants, and blessings.
   Abraham is justified by faith, while Isaac plays a pivotal role
   in the continuation of the promise and serves as a type within the history of redemption.
6. The Jacob Cycle (Chapters 25–36)
   > God’s election does not depend on human actions,
   but rather stems from His sovereign grace.
   Through struggles and transformation, Jacob is eventually renamed Israel,
   becoming the progenitor of the twelve tribes of Israel.
7. The Joseph Cycle (Chapters 37–50)
   > God uses human evil to accomplish His benevolent plan of redemption.
   Joseph is brought to Egypt, where he ultimately preserves Jacob’s entire family,
   ensuring their survival and setting the historical stage for the future Exodus.
