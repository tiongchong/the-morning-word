
# Genesis 1:9–13 — The Third Day: Dry Land Appears, Vegetation Emerges

**New American Standard Bible (NASB):**

> 1:9 Then God said, “Let the waters below the heavens be gathered into one place, and let the dry land appear”; and it was so.
>
> 1:10 And God called the dry land “earth,” and the gathering of the waters He called “seas”; and God saw that it was good.
>
> 1:11 Then God said, “Let the earth sprout vegetation, plants yielding seed, and fruit trees on the earth bearing fruit according to their kind with seed in them”; and it was so.
>
> 1:12 The earth produced vegetation, plants yielding seed according to their kind, and trees bearing fruit with seed in them, according to their kind; and God saw that it was good.
>
> 1:13 And there was evening and there was morning, a third day.

## I. The Placement of This Passage within the Literary Structure of Chapter 1

The first three days, which constitute the "establishment of realms" unit, reach their conclusion here:

- **Day 1**: Establishment of the realm of time (light/darkness, day and night).
- **Day 2**: Establishment of the upper spatial realm (the expanse/firmament, separating the waters above from the waters below).
- **Day 3**: Establishment of the terrestrial ecological realm (dry land and seas, along with plant life upon the earth).

Corresponding to this in a mirrored fashion, the "filling of the realms" during the latter three days culminates on the sixth day with the creation of land-dwelling living creatures and humanity.

A striking literary feature is the appearance of the phrase "God saw that it was good" twice on the third day—the only instance of such a double affirmation of "goodness" throughout the six days of creation. In Hebrew narrative, this serves as an emphatic marker, signaling the completion of a habitable realm on the earth’s surface and representing a major milestone in the establishment of order during the first three days. While the division of the aquatic environment was still underway on the second day, by the third day, the waters had receded, dry land had emerged, and vegetation had begun to grow. With the framework for a life-sustaining earth now firmly in place, God provides a twofold seal of approval upon the goodness of this order.

Looking back at the initial state described in Genesis 1:2—where the entire earth was covered by a vast expanse of water (the deep)—we see a progression: on the second day, the waters were separated vertically (into waters above and waters below); on the third day, they were gathered horizontally, causing the waters on the earth to collect and the dry land to appear. Through the power of His word, God continuously gathered, constrained, and partitioned the chaotic, primordial waters, step by step making way for land where life could dwell.

## II. In-depth Exegesis, Verse by Verse

### Part One: The Separation of Dry Land and Seas (1:9–10)

> And God said, “Let the waters under the heavens be gathered together into one place, and let the dry land appear.” And it was so. God called the dry land Earth, and the waters that were gathered together He called Seas. And God saw that it was good.

#### 1. “Let the waters under the heavens be gathered together into one place”

This passage once again reflects how the Book of Genesis reframes the motifs found in Ancient Near Eastern creation myths. In pagan narratives, the watery abyss is an untamable, hostile force; here, however, the text reveals that the ocean need not be defeated—at a single command from God, the scattered waters willingly converge into the specific area He has designated. Chaos possesses no independent will to resist the Creator’s decree.

The theological significance of being “gathered together into one place” is this: the bodies of water are not scattered at random; rather, they are assigned boundaries and a defined dwelling place. The seas have limits established by God (cf. Psalm 104:9: “You set a boundary that they may not pass over”). Only during the judgment of the Flood does God temporarily relax these boundaries—a detail that echoes the earlier reference to the “waters above” mentioned on the second day of creation.

#### 2. "Let the dry land appear"

The emergence of dry land (*erets*—the same word for "earth" used in the phrase "heavens and the earth" in 1:1) signifies the appearance of a stable platform where life can take root, dwell, and exercise dominion. Prior to this, there was no surface upon which to stand, plant, or build. The appearance of dry land on the third day serves as the geographical prerequisite for all subsequent terrestrial life, the Garden of Eden, and the mandate entrusted to humanity.

#### 3. "God called the dry land 'earth,' and the gathered waters He called 'seas'"

This follows the established pattern wherein "naming" signifies "sovereignty." By naming the earth and the seas, God declares that both belong to Him; He is the Master of both the land and the oceans. A progression is evident here: on the first day, God names the day and the night; on the second, the expanse (firmament); and on the third, the earth and the seas—with God progressively asserting His ownership over various dimensions of the entire created order.

Covenantal Theological Connection: The subsequent commission given to humanity to govern the earth (1:26–28) is predicated upon the fact that God already possesses the earth in its entirety. Humans are not the owners of the earth; rather, they act as stewards, governing the land that God has already named and established. Ownership belongs to the Creator, while the responsibility of stewardship is entrusted to those created in His image.

#### 4. The First "God Saw That It Was Good"

Regarding the demarcation between land and sea and the formation of the earth’s surface: the order established on the earth’s surface has fulfilled God’s purpose—the chaotic waters no longer cover everything, and habitable land has emerged.

### Part II: The Creation of Vegetation on the Earth (1:11–12)

> And God said, "Let the earth sprout vegetation: plants yielding seed, and fruit trees bearing fruit in which is their seed, each according to its kind, upon the earth." And it was so. The earth brought forth vegetation—plants yielding seed according to their own kinds, and trees bearing fruit in which is their seed, each according to its kind. And God saw that it was good.

#### 1. "Let the earth sprout..."

God’s command for the "earth" to produce vegetation does not imply that the earth possesses an independent creative power; rather, it means that God endowed the earth—which He Himself created—with an inherent reproductive potential. God instilled internal laws within the created order, enabling it to bring forth life in accordance with His command. This distinction is crucial: God is the ultimate source of all life, while the created order possesses a limited, divinely bestowed capacity to generate life.

The verb used here remains *‘asah* ("to make," "to bring about"), consistent with the linguistic pattern employed throughout the six days of ordering—distinct from the term *bara’* ("to create out of nothing") found in verse 1:1.

#### 2. The Progression of Three Layers of Vegetation: Grasses → Seed-bearing Plants → Fruit-bearing Trees

Life forms unfold from the simplest to the most complex, accompanied by the recurring phrase: **"according to its kind."**

"According to its kind" (*min*) is the core keyword in this chapter’s narrative of life. This same principle is reiterated later: for the living creatures in the waters and the birds on the fifth day, and for the livestock and wild beasts on the sixth day.

- **Theological Significance:** God established fixed boundaries for the "kinds" of life. Life propagates according to the order He ordained—it is not a formless, boundless, or fluid state of flux. The diversity of life exists alongside stable, divinely established limits. This serves as an extension of the overarching motif of "separation and boundaries."
- **Exegetical Note:** The text mentions that the fruit contains "seed within itself," indicating that the mechanism for reproduction was divinely embedded into created life. Living things possess an inherent potential to perpetuate their own kind—a feature that is part of God’s original design.

#### 3. The Second "God Saw That It Was Good"

This pronouncement concerns the establishment of the vegetation-based life system on the earth—an ecological framework capable of providing both food and habitats for living things, which has now fulfilled God’s intended purpose. With two distinct orders—the physical space of the earth’s surface and the vegetation upon it—now fully established, the third day concludes with a twofold declaration that it is "good."

### Part III: "And there was evening, and there was morning—the third day."

Following the Hebrew logic of timekeeping, the process begins in the dimness of twilight and arrives at the complete, ordered state of the earth’s surface as established by God. With this, the "establishment of realms"—the focus of the first three days—is complete: time, the expanse of the heavens, the separation of land and sea, and the emergence of vegetation. The stage is now fully set, awaiting the creatures that will, over the next three days, fill these respective realms.

## III. Synthesis: Connecting Core Themes Across Genesis 1–6

### 1. The Progression from Chaos to Order

The chaotic, all-encompassing waters described in Genesis 1:2 have—through the establishment of light and darkness (Day 1), the separation of waters above and below (Day 2), and the division of land from sea alongside the growth of vegetation (Day 3)—been transformed into a stable world capable of sustaining life. God’s work of establishing order proceeds steadily. The incursion of sin serves to erode this order, while the judgment of the Flood represents a reversal—a return of the ordered world to a state of chaotic, watery abyss.

### 2. Foreshadowing the Provision of Food

The seed-bearing plants and fruit-bearing trees mentioned on the third day directly anticipate God’s provision of vegetation for human consumption in Genesis 1:29. They also serve as the foundational context for the various trees in the Garden of Eden—including the Tree of the Knowledge of Good and Evil and the Tree of Life (as described in Genesis 2). God established the system of sustenance before assigning humanity their mission or granting permission regarding what to eat. The order of grace is always this: God first provides and supplies, and only then does He entrust a mission and grant the privilege of nourishment.

### 3. Preparing the Stage for Sabbath

The Sabbath of the seventh day was not established in a vacuum; it presupposes that God had already completed the formation and filling of the created order. The emergence of dry land and vegetation on the third day serves as a vital foundation for the Sabbath: with a stable earth and productive vegetation, there exists a space where one can rest and enjoy the goodness of God’s creation.

### 4. The Motif of Boundaries: "According to Their Kinds"

God’s act of setting boundaries for various kinds of life is consistent with His establishment of boundaries between light and darkness, the waters, and the sea and dry land. At its core, the conflict throughout the Book of Genesis stems from the desire of created beings to transgress the boundaries God has set. In the new creation (as depicted in the Book of Revelation), all boundaries find their perfect fulfillment in Christ, with no further risk of the collapse of these boundaries—a collapse once brought about by sin.

## IV. Clarifying Misconceptions in Biblical Interpretation

### Pitfall 1: "Plants appeared on the third day, yet the sun and moon did not appear until the fourth; since plants cannot survive without sunlight, this constitutes a contradiction in the Bible."

**Analysis:** The theological focus of this passage is to declare, first and foremost, that the ultimate sovereignty over life and the earth belongs to God—not to the sun. In God’s ordained plan, He could establish the earth and its vegetation first, and subsequently introduce the sun and moon—which govern day and night and serve as signs—as complementary instruments. The text is not a manual on the biological requirements for plant growth; rather, the narrative sequence is intentionally structured to dismantle pagan worship of the sun, moon, and stars. The sun and moon are not the masters of life; they are merely created instruments, fashioned by God to fulfill the order He had already established.

### Pitfall 2: "The phrase 'let the earth bring forth' suggests that the earth possesses the capacity to spontaneously evolve new species, thereby supporting an evolutionary perspective."

**Analysis:** The phrase "let the earth bring forth" refers to a process of emergence occurring under God’s command—a process strictly constrained by the boundary of "according to its kind." The text does not describe a fluid transition where species cross these boundaries; on the contrary, the expression "according to its kind" underscores the categorical limits established by God. One cannot isolate the phrase "let the earth bring forth" from the qualifying condition "according to its kind" in order to construct an evolutionary interpretation.

### Pitfall 3: The Double "Good" Is Merely a Scribal Repetition with No Special Literary Significance

**Analysis:** Hebrew narrative rarely contains meaningless repetition. The dual pronouncement of "good" indicates that the third day encompasses two major creative acts—the separation of sea and land, and the establishment of vegetation—and marks the completion of the initial three-day phase of setting up the structural domains. This is a deliberate narrative emphasis, not a scribal error.

## V. Perspective for Morning Meditation

The third day reveals that God’s work unfolds in stages; He does not rush to execute His entire plan at once, but rather builds the stage of order layer by layer. He prepares the earth before bringing forth its produce; He establishes the space before providing the resources for life. He is a God who makes provision in advance.

Furthermore, this passage reaffirms the blessing found in boundaries: God sets limits for the seas and defines the boundaries for various kinds of life. While we often yearn for a freedom "without limits," the creation narrative consistently reveals that the boundaries established by the Creator are not a form of deprivation, but rather the very foundation upon which life can exist securely, flourish, and be blessed.

> **Reflection Questions:** Do I recognize that all the stable environments and resources for survival in my life are graces prepared in advance by God? Do I respect the various boundaries He has established within my life?

## Summary

Genesis 1:9–13 (the third day) concludes the "establishment of realms" that began on the first day: waters gather to form seas, dry land emerges, and the earth brings forth vegetation—grass, plants, and fruit trees—each according to its kind. Twice, God pronounces these developments "good," marking the formal formation of a terrestrial ecosystem capable of sustaining life. With the stage-setting work of the first three days complete, the narrative now shifts to the next three days: the filling of these realms.
