
# Genesis 1:1–2: The Foundational Declaration of the Creation Narrative—From Absolute Origin to a Primal State Awaiting Order

**New American Standard Bible (NASB):**

> 1:1 In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth.
>
> 1:2 And the earth was a formless and desolate emptiness, and darkness was over the surface of the deep, and the Spirit of God was hovering over the surface of the waters.

---

## I. The Literary and Theological Positioning of This Passage within Genesis 1 and the Entire Bible

Genesis 1:1–2 serves as the opening anchor for the entire Hebrew Bible. Simultaneously, it establishes a dual-movement framework that undergirds the whole of the first chapter: a progression from an initial state—characterized as "unformed and unfilled"—toward an ordered created realm that is "shaped, filled, and very good."

### 1. Literary Function

Verse 1:1 serves as an introductory declaration, establishing an ontological distinction between the Creator and all created things. Verse 1:2 functions as a background description (a Hebrew "disjunctive" clause, rather than a sequence of narrative actions); it depicts the primordial state that existed after the creation of the heavens and the earth (1:1) but before the commencement of the six-day formative work. The actual, sequential narrative of the six days begins only at 1:3, with the words: "And God said, 'Let there be light.'"

This syntactic structure is crucial: verse 1:2 does not describe an "independent event that occurred after 1:1," but rather outlines the initial condition of the earth at the moment God began the six-day process of establishing order.

### 2. Apologetic Orientation (Countering Ancient Near Eastern Mythology)

In surrounding creation myths—such as the Babylonian *Enuma Elish*—the primordial waters or the "deep" (*tehôm*) are portrayed as chaotic deities that contend against the gods. In these accounts, the gods must defeat these chaotic forces in order to establish order in the world.

Genesis 1:1–2 directly reframes this narrative: the deep, the vast waters, and the unformed earth are all part of God’s creation; there are no chaotic deities that exist independently of the Creator or stand as His equals.

Chaos is not an adversary possessing autonomous authority; rather, it is a created state that has not yet been shaped by God’s command nor filled with life.

### 3. The Foundational Underpinning for the Redemptive Thread Throughout the Book of Genesis

The phrase "formless and void" (*tohu wabohu*) is used in later Old Testament passages (Jeremiah 4:23; Isaiah 34:11) to describe a state where order has collapsed and desolation has returned following the arrival of judgment.

This establishes a cyclical narrative:

- God brings an ordered world out of *tohu wabohu* (formlessness and void);
- Human rebellion causes the world to slide back toward desolation and chaos;
- The ultimate goal of the plan of redemption is to restore the order damaged by sin, arriving at the perfection of a new creation.

The narratives of the Flood and the Tower of Babel, as well as the promises given to the patriarchs, can all be traced back to this central theme: "order—disorder—restoration."

### 4. The Foundational Basis of Creation Theology

This passage establishes the underlying premise of *creatio ex nihilo* (creation out of nothing):

"In the beginning, God created the heavens and the earth (*bara’* — אֵת הַשָּׁמַיִם וְאֵת הָאָרֶץ)."

In the Old Testament, the verb *bara’* refers exclusively to a creative act by God that brings an entirely new dimension of existence into being—distinct from the act of fashioning or shaping (*asah*) from pre-existing materials.

## II. In-depth, Verse-by-Verse Analysis

### ✦ Genesis 1:1: In the beginning, God created the heavens and the earth

#### 1. "In the beginning (*bĕrēʾšît* בְּרֵאשִׁית)"

This is the opening term in Hebrew—a preposition combined with a noun—literally meaning "in the midst of the beginning."

- **An Important Distinction:** God Himself is eternal and has no beginning. "The beginning" does not refer to the beginning of God, but rather to the inception of the entire created order of space and time. Time itself is a dimension of the created realm; thus, "the beginning" marks the starting point for all measurable time, space, and matter.

- **Exegetical Perspectives:** Some Jewish exegetical traditions interpret this verse as a subordinate clause—"When God began to create the heavens and the earth, the earth was already formless and void..."—treating verse 1:1 as a piece of background information. Mainstream exegesis, however, maintains that 1:1 is an independent, declarative main clause: at the very inception of the created order, God brought the heavens and the earth into existence, while the subsequent six days involved the shaping and filling of this primordial material. The critical point of divergence between these two grammatical interpretations lies in how one understands the scope of the action denoted by the verb *bara’*.

#### 2. "Created (*bara’* בָּרָא)"

The first core theological verb in the opening chapter of the entire volume:

- The subject is always God; nowhere in the Old Testament can a created being perform the action of *bara’*.
- Meaning: To bring something into entirely new existence, rather than processing pre-existing raw materials. This serves as the primary Old Testament lexical foundation for the doctrine of *creatio ex nihilo* ("creation out of nothing"). It is distinct from the term *asah*—frequently used throughout the subsequent six days—which denotes making, shaping, or arranging materials that already exist.

**Key Synthesis:**

In verse 1:1, *bara’* refers to the creation of the very substance of the heavens and the earth; in the six days that follow, *asah* is used to describe the work of establishing order, making distinctions, and filling. These are not two separate acts of creation, but rather a single, original creative act, followed by a process of shaping and perfecting over the course of six days.

#### 3. "The heavens and the earth" (*hashamayim veha’aretz* — הַשָּׁמַיִם וְאֵת הָאָרֶץ)

This is a Hebrew merism (a figure of speech using two contrasting terms to represent a whole). It does not refer merely to "the sky" and "the ground" as separate entities, but serves as a comprehensive term for the entirety of created reality—equivalent to "all created things." It encompasses both the visible and invisible realms of creation and is not limited solely to the physical universe.

**Theological Implication:**

There is no primordial "chaotic matter" that exists independently of "the heavens and the earth" or outside of God’s creative act. The waters, the deep, and the foundational substance of the earth—described later in verse 1:2—are already encompassed within the scope of the creation of "the heavens and the earth" mentioned in verse 1:1.

This directly refutes a dualistic view of creation, which posits that eternal, chaotic matter existed beforehand and that God merely stepped in to organize it.

### ✦ Genesis 1:2 The earth was formless and void, and darkness was over the face of the deep; and the Spirit of God was hovering over the face of the waters

#### 1. "The earth was formless and void (*tohu wabohu* תֹהוּ וָבֹהוּ)"

A Hebrew alliterative, paired phrase—a signature expression of this chapter:

- ***tohu***: Lacking definite form, desolate, uninhabitable, devoid of a stable structure.
- ***bohu***: Empty, lacking internal content or the presence of life.

**Core Characterization:**

This describes a state that has been ontologically created by God, yet remains functionally unformed and unfilled with life. It does not represent moral evil, nor does it signify an adversarial force independent of God.

Isaiah 45:18 provides a crucial point of contrast: "He who created the earth... did not create it to be *tohu* [formless/empty], but formed it to be inhabited." God’s ultimate purpose in creation was not a state of formlessness and void, but a world that is habitable, orderly, and teeming with life. *Tohu wabohu* is merely an intermediate, preparatory stage within the creative process—not God’s final intent.

#### 2. "Darkness was over the face of the deep (*choshek al pney tehom* חֹשֶׁךְ עַל־פְּנֵי תְהוֹם)"

- ***tehom* (the deep/the great deep):** The primordial waters. In Ancient Near Eastern mythology, this is often personified as a chaotic sea monster; however, Genesis demotes it to a mere body of water created by God—one that simply had not yet been assigned its boundaries (which would occur on the third day, when the waters were gathered into "seas").

- **Darkness:** Likewise, this is not an evil spiritual force. It is simply a created state that existed before light had been separated from it. Later in the chapter, when God "separates the light from the darkness" and "calls the light Day, and the darkness Night," He is establishing order within this state of darkness—He is not engaging in a battle against dark, malevolent powers.

**Apologetic Points:**

In pagan creation narratives, the dramatic conflict centers on "deities versus the chaotic abyss/darkness." In contrast, the Genesis account contains no narrative of gods battling chaos; instead, it presents the sovereign word of God, which acts upon the created material—already belonging to Him—to separate and fashion it.

#### 3. "The Spirit of God was hovering over the face of the waters (*ruach elohim merahefet al pney hamayim* רוּחַ אֱלֹהִים מְרַחֶ֖פֶת עַל־פְּנֵ֥י הַמָּֽיִם)"

- **ruach elohim:** The Spirit of God / the breath of God. In this Old Testament passage, we find a foreshadowing of the doctrine of the Trinity through progressive revelation: the work of creation is not solely that of God the Father; the Holy Spirit is likewise actively present, sustaining and preparing the created material, awaiting the command of the Word.

- **merahefet (hovering/brooding, sheltering with wings):** This verb carries the imagery of gentle protection and incubation—a far cry from violent conflict. The Holy Spirit hovers over the unformed waters, guarding this primordial created material and preparing it for the order and life that will follow the utterance of God’s word.

**✅ A Crucial Theological Point:**

Even when the world existed in an unformed state—*tohu wabohu*—the Spirit of God was already actively present. At no point has the created order ever existed apart from His presence and providence. There was never a period characterized by "the absence of God" or a state where chaos reigned independently.

## III. Intertextual Synthesis: The Origins of Core Motifs in Genesis 1–6

### 1. The Origin of the Motif of "Separation/Distinction" (*badal*)

The central action throughout the six days of creation in Genesis 1 is God’s repeated act of "separating"—dividing light from darkness, the waters above from the waters below, and the seas from the dry land—thereby establishing boundaries and order.

Genesis 1:1–2 provides the starting point for this process of "separation": upon a primordial, undifferentiated foundation of water and earth, God initiates a work of progressive distinction.

Boundaries, distinctions, and ordered hierarchies are all gifts from the Creator. The essence of sin lies in the creature’s active attempt to tear down the boundaries established by God (as seen in the human desire to "be like God" in chapter 3, or the corruption resulting from the blurring of boundaries in chapter 6).

### 2. The Foundation of the "Forming and Filling" Symmetry

- **The First Three Days:** The "forming" of the stage (separating light from darkness, dividing the waters, and distinguishing the seas from the dry land, which then brings forth vegetation).
- **The Last Three Days:** The "filling" of the stage (the sun, moon, and stars fill the realms of light and darkness; birds and sea creatures fill the skies and waters; living creatures and humans fill the dry land).

The starting point for this perfectly symmetrical structure is the state of *tohu wabohu*—"unformed and unfilled"—described in verse 2.

The narrative of the entire first chapter traces a progression from the void and chaos of verse 2 to the ultimate fulfillment in verse 31, where "God saw all that he had made, and it was very good."

### 3. The Dual Imagery of Water (A Medium for Life / An Instrument of Judgment)

In verse 2, the waters—hovered over by the Spirit of God—serve as the primordial material destined to sustain abundant life. Yet, following the Fall, these same waters become an instrument of judgment (the Flood in Genesis 6–9), returning the world to a desolate state reminiscent of *tohu wabohu*. Following the Flood, in the covenant with Noah, God once again establishes boundaries for the waters, restoring an ordered, habitable world. The dual imagery of water is inscribed, from the very first stroke, upon a water surface with a 1:2 ratio.

### 4. The Foundational Context of the Covenant of Creation

The Covenant of Creation (or Covenant of Works) presupposes that God prepared an orderly, governable world, and then entrusted the mission of stewardship to human beings, who were created in His image.

Furthermore, verses 1:1–2 reveal that God brought the created order into existence—freely and from a source of absolute perfection. Everything entrusted to humanity for stewardship belongs entirely to the Creator; as stewards, human beings possess no capital or resources independent of the Creator.

## IV. Clarifying Common Exegetical Misconceptions

### Pitfall 1: The view that the *tohu wabohu* (formless and void) in verse 1:2 represents the ruins of a previously perfect world—destroyed by divine judgment following the fall of the angels (the "Restitution Theory" or "Gap Theory")

**Analysis:**

The "Gap Theory" posits that God created a perfect world in verse 1:1, which was subsequently destroyed—becoming the chaotic, ruined state described in verse 1:2—after the angels sinned; the six days of creation are thus interpreted as a second, restorative act of creation.

- **Grammatical Flaw:** Verse 1:2 functions as a descriptive, circumstantial clause providing background information; it is not a sequential narrative statement (i.e., it does not mean "and then the earth *became* formless and void"). The original Hebrew text contains no verb indicating a transition into a state of ruin.

- **Scriptural Evidence:** Isaiah 45:18 explicitly states that God created the earth to be inhabited. His original plan for creation inherently included a process of moving from an unformed state toward a fully fashioned, complete state. There is no mention of the fall of the angels or any prior judgment anywhere in the first chapter of Genesis. Mainstream biblical scholarship rejects the Gap Theory.

### Pitfall 2: The view that *tohu wabohu* refers to primordial matter that has existed eternally, and that God merely organized this pre-existing chaos

**Analysis:**

This directly contradicts the declaration in verse 1:1—that God *bara’* (created) the heavens and the earth. All matter, the deep, the waters, and the foundations of the earth were created by God in verse 1:1; there is no independent raw material that is co-eternal with God—to suggest otherwise is to fall into the trap of dualism.

### Pitfall 3: "The *ruach*—the 'Spirit of God'—is merely a 'great wind,' not the personal Holy Spirit."

**Analysis:**

While *ruach* can be translated as "wind," the imagery of the verb *merahefet* (hovering/brooding over) suggests active care. Furthermore, the progressive revelation throughout the Old Testament and the New Testament’s confirmation of the Holy Spirit’s role in creation (Psalm 104:30) support the view that this refers to the active, providential care of God’s personal Spirit. Reducing this to a mere natural wind weakens the theological weight of the passage, which declares God’s active presence at the very inception of creation.

### Pitfall 4: "The 'beginning' can be understood as the temporal starting point of the Big Bang—exactly equivalent to the beginning of time as defined by modern physics."

**Analysis:**

The "beginning" mentioned in the text refers to the inception of the created order within the framework of biblical theology. The purpose of the text is not to provide a physical model for the origin of the universe, but to proclaim the sovereignty of the Creator. While it may engage in dialogue with scientific models, the text should not be reduced to a mere footnote or commentary on physical hypotheses.

## V. Morning Meditation: Practical Application

### 1. Meditating on the Sovereignty of the Creator

God existed before any created form came into being. All the order, life, and the world that we behold did not emerge spontaneously; rather, they are the result of His creation.

Even in stages that appear unformed or desolate, His Spirit is already present—hovering, watching over, and preparing. Likewise, the periods of "emptiness and chaos" in our own lives—those times of waiting—remain under the shelter of His hovering presence.

### 2. Meditation on the value of order boundaries

God's work of creation is to establish beautiful distinctions and boundaries layer by layer from the original state of no distinction; boundaries are not prisons, but the prerequisite for an abundant life.

We often long to break through the boundaries set by God to gain "freedom", but the creation narrative reveals that to break away from the boundaries set by the Creator is to return to tohu wabohu-style desolation.

### 3. Meditation on hope

God never sees the unformed state as an end; His purpose is always to bring emptiness and chaos to a "very good" perfection.

This also points us to the hope of redemption: the disintegration of order brought about by sin will not be the final outcome, and God has the power to bring all disorder back into His perfect order.

---

## VI. Summary and Direction of Narrative Advancement

Genesis 1:1‑2 sets the starting point for the entire first chapter of the book: God created the heavens and the earth from nothing. The original creation was in an unformed and unfilled state. The Holy Spirit covered the waters, waiting for the shaping work of God’s word.

The narrative then advances to 1:3‑5, the first day: the creation of light, the distinction and naming of light and darkness.
