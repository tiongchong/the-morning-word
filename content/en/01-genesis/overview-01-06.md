
# Genesis 1–6: An Overview of Literary Structure and Biblical-Theological Framework

**Perspective:** Covenant Theology and Biblical Narrative Theology, incorporating an analysis of Hebrew narrative literary features and intertextual comparisons with Ancient Near Eastern texts.

**Core Themes:** The Created Order — The Missional Mandate of the *Imago Dei* — The Covenantal Trust — The Intrusion of Sin — The Divergence of Lineages — The Impending Judgment.

**Objective:** To serve as a roadmap for our upcoming verse-by-verse, in-depth study; and to distinguish between the surface-level literary structure and the underlying logic of the redemptive narrative.

---

## Defining the Boundaries: The Original Literary Structure of the Ancient Hebrew Text

The "chapters" referred to here are divisions added by later generations; they are not the original, inherent separators found in the ancient Hebrew manuscripts.

The Hebrew text employs the following literary markers to delineate narrative units:

- Repetitive sentence structures
- Heading-like introductions
- Genealogies (*toledoth*, meaning "accounts of the descendants/history of...")
- Shifts in scene
- Clusters of key words

These constitute the literary framework inherent to the text as originally composed by Moses.

A hallmark literary marker throughout the Book of Genesis is *toledoth* (תּוֹלְדוֹת—"these are the descendants/history of..."). Appearing ten times, these markers serve as the structural hinges for the entire book; the first two instances of *toledoth* are found within chapters 1–6.

## I. Broad Divisions of Genesis 1–6

### Unit A: The Prologue to Creation (Genesis 1:1–2:3)

Theme: The six days of creation + the seventh day of rest.

- A general prologue preceding the first *toledoth*.
- The establishment of the cosmic order.

### Unit B: The Garden of Eden Narrative (Genesis 2:4–3:24)

Theme: A second account of the creation of humanity; the setting for the covenant in the Garden; temptation and the Fall; the curse; the promise of the woman’s offspring.

- 3:15: The seed of the Gospel.

> Genesis 2:4 contains the first *toledoth* in the entire book: "This is the account of the heavens and the earth when they were created, in the day that the Lord God made the earth and the heavens."
>
> It serves as a transitional heading, shifting the focus from the macro-level creation of the universe to the specific setting of the covenant involving humanity.
>
> Many people mistakenly assume that chapter 1...

### Unit C: The Narrative of Two Lineages (Genesis 4:1–5:32)

Theme: The divergence of two human lines.

#### Chapter 4: The Lineage of Cain

- A rebellious progeny.
- Urban civilization.
- Development of skills and crafts.
- Self-glorification.
- A culture of violence.

#### Chapter 5: The Lineage of Seth

- *Toledoth* #2: The account of the descendants of Adam (5:1).
- Calling upon the name of the LORD.
- The continuation of the line of the promised offspring.
- The cycle of death (the recurring refrain "and he died"), leading up to Noah.

### Unit D: The Fullness of Corruption and the Announcement of the Flood (Genesis 6:1–22)

Themes:

- The great wickedness of humanity on the earth.
- The narrative of the "sons of God" intermarrying with the "daughters of men."
- The earth filled with violence.
- Noah finds grace.
- Preparation for the covenant of the ark.

## II. Detailed Literary Structural Analysis by Unit

(Including recurring motifs, contrasting structures, key terms, and theological functions)

### Unit A | Genesis 1:1–2:3: The Six-Day Creation Narrative

Theme: A symmetrical, poetic structure.

#### 1. Overarching Opening Statement

Genesis 1:1:

"In the beginning, God created the heavens and the earth."

#### 2. Transitional Descriptive Statement

Genesis 1:2:

Formlessness and emptiness (chaos), darkness over the deep, and the Spirit of God hovering over the face of the waters.

#### 3. The Symmetrical Structure of the Six-Day Creation

The six days of creation form two parallel sets of three:

- The first three days establish "domains."
- The subsequent three days fill those domains.

This is a classic example of a symmetrical "mirror" structure, characteristic of Hebrew narrative.

##### First Set: Establishing Spaces/Domains

- Day 1: Light; separation of light from darkness → Establishment of temporal order (day and night).
- Day 2: Separation of the waters above from the waters below; creation of the expanse (firmament) → Establishment of spatial order (between the heavens and the earth).
- Day 3: Separation of water from dry land; emergence of vegetation → Establishment of ecological order (land and plant life).

##### Second Set: Filling the Previously Established Domains

- Day 4: Sun, moon, and stars—to govern day and night and serve as signs → Filling the domain of light and darkness established on Day 1.
- Day 5: Living creatures in the waters and birds in the air → Filling the expanse and the waters established on Day 2.
- Day 6: Livestock, wild beasts, and humans—with humans created in the image of God and entrusted with dominion over the entire earth → Filling the domain of dry land established on Day 3.

#### 4. Climax and Conclusion

Genesis 2:1–3:

On the seventh day, God rested and set it apart as a holy day.

#### 5. Recurring Literary Markers

A rhythmic refrain that runs throughout this unit:

> “And God said... and it was so. God saw that it was good. And there was evening, and there was morning—the [X]th day.”

This is a formalized, liturgical rhythm—resembling a doxology used in temple worship—which declares that:

- God’s word brings about order.
- His sovereign pronouncements serve as the laws for the created order.
- “Good” signifies that something aligns with the purpose He established and exists within the framework of His covenantal order.

#### 6. Theological Function

It establishes the cosmic stage for the **Covenant of Creation (Covenant of Works)**.

The creation of humanity, the mandate to exercise dominion, and the call to rest are all grounded in this.

#### 7. Foreshadowing for What Follows

Rest is a blessing; by remaining within this established order, humanity enters into rest. Sin, conversely, is a departure from this order, resulting in the forfeiture of that rest.

### Unit B | Genesis 2:4–3:24: The Garden of Eden Narrative

Theme: A self-contained, covenantal courtroom scene—a miniature tragedy built on the contrast between opposing sides.

Moving away from the grand, distant, and regal title "Elohim" (used in Chapter 1), this passage frequently employs the compound name "YHWH Elohim" (the LORD God).

The introduction of this covenantal, self-existent name into the Garden serves as a literary signal:

A transition from the creation of the cosmos to personal, relational, covenantal engagement.

#### 1. Structural Outline

##### 1. 2:4–7: Transitional Heading (*toledoth*) + A Close-up on the Creation of Humanity

- Dust + the breath of life, imparted by YHWH.
- In contrast to the royal decree "Let Us make man" found in Chapter 1, this account emphasizes humanity’s dependent nature—that our very breath originates from God.

##### 2. 2:8–15: Preparing the Garden

- Placing Adam in the garden.
- Entrusting him with the tasks of keeping and tending it.
- A stewardship mandate—a concrete expression of the "image-bearing" mission described in Chapter 1.

##### 3. 2:16–17: The Covenantal Command

- Permission to eat from any fruit in the garden.
- Prohibition against eating from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil.
- The consequence: death on the day it is eaten.
- This constitutes the probationary term of the Covenant of Works—a clear test of obedience, and a condition set within a context of grace.

##### 4. 2:18–24: The Institution of Marriage

- It is not good for man to be alone.
- The creation of a companion (spouse).
- The two become one flesh.
- A created fellowship—a wonderful gift from God.

##### 5. 2:25: The Narrative’s Baseline of Peace

- The two are naked, yet feel no shame.
- An original state devoid of sin or estrangement.

##### 6. 3:1–7: The Scene of Temptation

- The serpent as a cunning creature.
- A three-part dialogue:
  - The serpent questions God’s word.
  - Eve distorts God’s command.
  - The act of eating, followed by sharing with Adam.
- Their eyes are opened; shame enters, and estrangement begins.

##### 7. 3:8–13: The Divine Courtroom: The Lord Descends for Judgment

- The humans hide.
- Evasion of responsibility.
- Mutual accusation (Adam blames the woman; the woman blames the serpent).

##### 8. 3:14–19: The Pronouncement of the Curse

- The curse upon the serpent.
- The hardships of childbirth and the dynamics of marital authority.
- The curse upon the ground; labor becomes necessary to obtain food.

##### 9. 3:15: The First Gospel Promise in the Entire Book

- The *Protoevangelium* (the first gospel).
- "He [the woman’s offspring] shall bruise your head, and you shall bruise his heel."
- This serves as the pivot point for the redemptive thread running through the entire book of Genesis and the whole of the Old Testament.

##### 10. 3:20–24: The Covering of Grace

- Garments of skin, prefiguring substitutionary atonement through the shedding of blood.
- Expulsion from the Garden of Eden.
- The placement of cherubim to guard the Tree of Life.
- Humanity cannot seize eternal life while in a state of sin; the path to redemption must be opened by God Himself.

#### 2. Core Contrast Structure

The author intentionally sets up the following:

- Obedience → Rest, absence of shame, enjoyment of the Tree of Life.
- Disobedience → Shame, hiding, toil, separation from the Tree of Life.
- The Word of God serves as the standard for life.
- The serpent’s craftiness begins by questioning: "Did God really say...?"

#### 3. Literary Points

The garden serves as an archetype of a sanctuary; the Garden of Eden was the first such sanctuary.

It is a place where God meets with His covenant representatives—a motif that continues through:

- The Tabernacle.
- The Temple.
- The New Jerusalem.

### Unit C | Genesis 4:1–5:32: A Narrative of Two Contrasting Lineages

Theme: Two diverging paths in human history.

This is a classic example of the dual-track, contrapuntal narrative style found in Hebrew literature; it should not be viewed merely as two separate genealogies.

#### 1. Chapter 4: The Line of Cain—A Lineage of Rebellion

##### Events

- Cain, driven by jealousy, murders Abel.
- Cain is cursed and becomes a wanderer.
- The first city is built.
- Descendants develop animal husbandry, musical instruments, and metallurgy (working with bronze and iron).
- Civilization, craftsmanship, and culture are not inherently evil, but here they develop under an autonomy that is detached from God’s order.
- Lamech’s "Song of Vengeance" represents the pinnacle of self-assertive violence.

##### Key Themes

- Building cities.
- Self-naming.
- Self-protection (relying on one’s own means).
- Violence.

#### 2. Genesis 4:25–26: A Turning Point

Adam begets Seth:

> “At that time, people began to call upon the name of the Lord.”

This marks the divergence of two paths:

- One pursuing self-glory and an autonomous civilization.
- The other calling upon God and awaiting the promised offspring.

#### 3. Chapter 5: *Toledoth* #2—The Account of Adam’s Descendants

Genesis 5:1:

“This is the account of the descendants of Adam.”

The genealogy of Seth, tracing from Adam to Noah.

##### A Recurring Formula

> “...lived ... years, and fathered ...; he lived another ... years, and had other sons and daughters; so all the days ... were ... years, and he died.”

The repeated phrase “and he died” serves as a somber, recurring literary refrain, making the consequences of the curse in Chapter 3 tangible:

Death entered humanity through sin, passed down from generation to generation, until the turning point reached with Noah.

#### 4. Theological Function

Addressing a question:

After the Fall, God did not immediately annihilate the entire human race; instead, He preserved a lineage—a line of the promised offspring—amidst a corrupt humanity.

Election did not begin with Abraham; it began as early as the time of Adam and Seth.

#### 5. Foreshadowing

The two lines will converge in chapter 6, both becoming corrupt.

A lineage characterized by "calling upon the name of the Lord" cannot be sustained by human effort alone; the corruption of humanity inevitably infiltrates it.

---

### Unit D | Genesis 6:1–22: The Fullness of Corruption and the Election of Noah by Grace

Theme: A narrative marking the critical threshold before judgment.

Literarily, this passage serves as a prologue to the Flood narrative, synthesizing all the motifs from chapters 1–5 and bringing the tension between the two previously distinct lineages to a climax.

#### 1. Genesis 6:1–4: The Sons of God and the Daughters of Men

> "The sons of God saw that the daughters of men were beautiful, and they took as wives any they chose."

##### Exegetical Perspective

Traditional Christian interpretation: The "sons of God" refer to the lineage of Seth—the descendants who worshiped God—while the "daughters of men" refer to women from the lineage of Cain.

The implication: The two previously separate lineages merged; the line of those who worshiped God was assimilated by the line of the worldly and rebellious, causing the boundaries of faith to vanish.

Sacred boundaries were breached; humanity was no longer set apart as holy, and corruption spread across the entire earth.

#### 2. Genesis 6:5: The Core Verdict

The theological centerpiece of this chapter:

> The Lord saw that the wickedness of man was great on the earth, and that every intent of the thoughts of his heart was only evil continually.

#### 3. Genesis 6:6–7: The Lord’s Grief of Heart

- The Lord felt grief in His heart.
- He regretted having made man on the earth.

This is an anthropopathic narrative—using the language of human emotion to describe God’s abhorrence of sin. It does not imply that God changed His plan; rather, it signifies that the purpose of the created order had been ruined by sin.

Following this, He declares His intent to destroy the humans and living creatures He had created.

#### 4. Genesis 6:8: A Sharp, Pivotal Statement

> But Noah found favor in the eyes of the Lord.

This serves as the fulcrum of grace for the entire passage.

Amidst a declaration of total judgment, a single remnant is chosen—carrying forward the line of promise that originated in the lineage of Seth.

#### 5. Genesis 6:9–22: *Toledoth* #3—The Account of Noah’s Descendants

Genesis 6:9:

“This is the account of Noah’s descendants.”

Content:

- Introduction of Noah as a righteous man who walked with God.
- God reveals His plan of judgment to Noah.
- Instruction to build the ark.
- Preparation for the covenant to be established through the ark.
- Noah carries out everything God commanded.

#### 6. Summary of Motifs: Where all the threads from chapters 1–6 converge

- The “goodness” of creation → After the intrusion of sin, the whole earth becomes filled with evil.
- The *Imago Dei* mandate (to rule and keep/guard) → Humanity, conversely, destroys the earth and kills one another.
- The promise of the offspring (3:15) → Temporarily narrowed down to a single remnant—Noah; the thread of redemption hangs upon one man.
- Order vs. Chaos → Judgment entails reverting a corrupted order back into chaos (the Flood represents a return to the chaotic, watery state described in Genesis 1:2).

## III. List of Unifying Narrative Motifs Across Chapters 1–6

(We will frequently refer back to these during our in-depth study.)

### 1. The Motif of the Word

God’s Word brings life and order; humanity’s rejection or distortion of God’s Word brings death and chaos.

### 2. The Motif of the Offspring (Seed)

One of the primary threads in biblical theology:

- The promised offspring (3:15) versus the rebellious offspring.
- Two competing lines of offspring in constant conflict.
- Culminating in Christ as the ultimate Offspring (Galatians 3:16).

### 3. The Motif of Separation/Boundaries

- Creation is an act of separation (light/darkness, waters/waters, dry land/seas).
- Sin is the erasure of the boundaries established by God.

### 4. The Motif of Rest

Rest on the seventh day is a blessing; sin causes humanity to lose this rest, leading to toil and heavy burdens.

### 5. Typology of Covering/Atonement

Garments of skin (3:21)—where the shedding of blood is required to cover shame—serve as a type (foreshadowing) of sacrifice and the cross of Christ.

### 6. The Cycle of Chaos and Order

- Creation: Chaos → Order.
- Sin: Order → Corruption.
- Judgment: Corruption → Chaos.
- Grace: Chaos gives rise to a new order (e.g., the Noahic Covenant following the Flood).

---

## IV. Structural Summary: Transitioning to a Detailed Exegetical Study

Chapters 1–6 cover the establishment and subsequent breaking of the Covenant of Works, as well as the preservation of a remnant of grace amidst the threat of judgment.

This is not merely a collection of disjointed creation accounts and ancient legends; rather, it is a coherent, progressive narrative argument:

1. God’s good creation.
2. A covenantal test given to humanity.
3. Human autonomy and rebellion, which introduce a curse and death.
4. God’s faithfulness to His promises, preserving a lineage of descendants throughout the generations.
5. The arrival of judgment when the earth becomes fully corrupted, alongside the provision of an ark—an act of grace to preserve a new generation of created beings.
6. The laying of the groundwork for the Noahic Covenant (chapter 9) and the Abrahamic Covenant (chapter 12).
