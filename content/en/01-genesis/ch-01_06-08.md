
# Genesis 1:6–8 — Day Two: The Expanse Separating the Waters

**New American Standard Bible (NASB):**

> 1:6 Then God said, “Let there be an expanse in the midst of the waters, and let it separate the waters from the waters.”
>
> 1:7 God made the expanse, and separated the waters that were below the expanse from the waters that were above the expanse; and it was so.
>
> 1:8 God called the [i]expanse “heaven.” And there was evening and there was morning, a second day.

---

## I. The Placement of This Passage within the Literary Structure of Chapter 1

Let us revisit the structure of the first three days, which focus on the "establishment of realms":

- **Day 1:** The establishment of the realm of time (day/night, light/darkness).
- **Day 2:** The establishment of the realm of space (the expanse/heavens separating the waters above from the waters below).
- **Day 3:** The establishment of the realms of land, sea, and vegetation.

The subsequent three days serve to "fill" these realms: Day 5, for instance, introduces birds and aquatic creatures, which fill the expanse and the waters established on Day 2.

A notable narrative detail is that while the phrase "God saw that it was good" appears explicitly on the first, third, fourth, fifth, and sixth days, it is absent from the original text of the second day. This is a deliberate feature of the Hebrew narrative, not an omission by a scribe. Various exegetical perspectives offer explanations:

1. **The Motif of Water and Judgment by Flood:** The "waters above" and the deep waters—separated on the second day—retain a latent motif of judgment by flood. In biblical narrative, water carries a dual symbolism: it is both a created element and a chaotic force associated with judgment. It is only on the third day, when dry land emerges and a fully habitable created order is established, that the declaration "it was good" appears.

2. **Literary Grouping:** The work of the second day and the separation of waters on the third day belong to a single, coherent sequence concerning the order of the aquatic environment. The pronouncement that it is "good" is reserved for the point at which the distinction between the aquatic environment and the dry land is fully realized. Regardless of which interpretation one adopts, the core remains the same: the second day serves as a pivotal stage in establishing the boundaries of spatial dimensions, as God systematically contains and delimits the chaotic waters that once covered everything.

---

## II. In-depth Exegesis, Verse by Verse

### 1. "And God said, 'Let there be an expanse between the waters, to separate water from water.'"

In the original Hebrew, the term is better translated as "expanse" (or "firmament") rather than the word for "air" (as found in the Chinese Union Version). This aligns with the translation: "Let there be an expanse between the waters, to separate the waters."

The Hebrew root for "expanse" conveys the idea of ​​something "firm" or "spread out." It reflects how the ancient Israelites envisioned a vast, arched space separating the waters below from the waters above—a concept distinct from the "atmosphere" as understood in modern physics.

> ⚠️ **Key Hermeneutical Distinction:** The text employs a *phenomenological* description—portraying the cosmos from the perspective of an observer on the ground—rather than presenting an ancient scientific model of the universe. The primary intent is not to teach celestial mechanics, but to declare that even the vast waters covering the earth are not beyond control; God possesses the authority to set boundaries—upper and lower limits—for these waters.

Returning to the initial state described in Genesis 1:2, the entire creation was enveloped by vast waters—the "deep" was covered in water. God’s first spatial command was to establish a division within this continuous expanse of water, creating two distinct regions: the waters below the expanse and the waters above the expanse.

**A Biblical-Theological Motif:** The "vast waters" serve as the primary embodiment of the *tohu wabohu* (chaos/formlessness). In Ancient Near Eastern mythology—specifically the Babylonian creation myth—the deep, chaotic waters are personified by the deity Tiamat, an adversary who must be subdued through a violent, bloody conflict. Genesis subverts this motif: God requires no battle; by His mere command, He establishes boundaries for the waters. Chaos possesses no power to resist or rebel; it is entirely subject to the word of the Creator. ### 2. "And God made the expanse, and separated the waters which were under the expanse from the waters which were above the expanse; and it was so."

**Regarding the verbs:** The term used here is *asah* ("made"), rather than *bara’* ("created")—a word reserved exclusively for God’s act of bringing something into existence out of nothing (as seen in verse 1:1). This aligns with the distinction noted earlier: *bara’* emphasizes the bringing of a new entity into existence, whereas *asah* focuses on shaping, dividing, and arranging materials that have already been created.

Verse 1:1 established the existence of the heavens and the earth (including the waters). The work of the six days, beginning from the first, consists largely of *asah*—the process of establishing order and setting boundaries.

"And it was so" serves as a recurring confirmatory formula throughout the first chapter: once God issues a command, reality immediately conforms to His will—there is no resistance, delay, or failure. This reinforces a foundational premise of the entire book: the laws governing the created order are a manifestation of God’s will.

### 3. "And God called the expanse 'heavens'."

This continues the paradigm established on the first day, where "naming" signifies an "assertion of sovereignty." By naming the expanse "heavens," God declares that this vast space—which separates the waters above from the waters below—belongs to Him; He is the Master of this realm.

The word for "heavens" here is the same as that used in the phrase "heavens and the earth" in verse 1:1, though the dimensions differ. In 1:1, "the heavens" refers to the entire celestial realm; in 1:8, the term specifically denotes the expanse established on the second day—the space separating the waters, which would later become the domain where birds fly (as noted in Genesis 1:20, where birds are described as flying "across the face of the expanse of the heavens"). This creates a literary correspondence between the two passages.

### 4. "And there was evening and there was morning, a second day."

This follows the Hebrew logic of timekeeping—moving from dusk toward dawn, and from chaotic darkness toward the ordered space established by God—maintaining a consistent narrative rhythm with the account of the first day.

## III. Synthesis: Connecting Core Themes in Genesis 1–6

### 1. The Progression of the Theme of Separation and Boundaries

On Day 1, God establishes temporal boundaries (light/darkness); on Day 2, He sets spatial boundaries (the expanse, or firmament, separating the waters above from the waters below); and on Day 3, He defines the boundaries between land and sea.

God’s process of establishing order is, in essence, the layering of stable boundaries. Conversely, the core act of sin is the persistent transgression of these divinely ordained boundaries: the eating of the forbidden fruit in the Garden of Eden, Cain’s violation of the sanctity of life by murdering his brother, the erosion of spiritual boundaries in the generation described in Genesis 6, and the attempt at the Tower of Babel to breach the boundary between heaven and earth. This thread of opposition runs consistently from the first chapter through the narrative of the Tower of Babel in chapter 11.

### 2. Water and the Foreshadowing of Judgment

The "waters above the expanse" serve as a biblical foreshadowing of the Flood. When the Flood of Noah’s time arrives, "the floodgates of the heavens were opened" (Genesis 7:11); the waters—previously held in check above the expanse—burst across their boundaries and poured down. In this sense, judgment is God’s temporary withdrawal of the constraints He once established, allowing the chaotic, primordial waters to surge back upon the earth.

**👉 The Biblical-Theological Chain:**

- **Creation =** The containment of the chaotic waters.
- **The Flood Judgment =** The loosening of these constraints, allowing the waters to return.
- **The Noahic Covenant (Post-Flood) =** God re-establishes boundaries, promising never again to destroy the earth completely by a flood (Genesis 9:11).

The division of the waters on the second day serves as a distant foreshadowing of the Flood narrative.

### 3. Spatial Preparation for the Mandate to Rule

By creating the expanse as a distinct realm, God provides the stage for the creation of birds on the fifth day, and grants humanity the capacity to behold the vast heavens while looking upward from the earth. Humanity’s mandate to exercise dominion—in accordance with the image of God—is grounded in a stable spatial order pre-established by God. Without the spatial boundaries set by God, there would be no created realm for humans to steward and govern.

### 4. The Foundation for the Order of Rest

The cycle of day and night serves as the fundamental rhythm underlying both labor and rest in the temporal dimension; the stable space, delineated by the firmament, provides the spatial foundation where humanity can dwell securely and enter into the rest of the seventh day.

Rest is more than merely ceasing from work in terms of time; it is, fundamentally, dwelling within the stable, ordered, and divinely demarcated space of God’s creation.

## IV. Clarifying Common Misinterpretations

### Pitfall 1: "There is an actual solid shell of water encasing the Earth above the firmament; the Bible describes a three-tiered cosmic structure."

**Clarification:**

This is a misreading that mistakes the Bible’s phenomenological and figurative language for an ancient scientific model of the cosmos.

Moses employed spatial imagery intelligible to the culture of his time—not to describe planetary atmospheric structures, but to proclaim God’s sovereignty over the waters and the entire expanse.

Insisting on a literal physical construction obscures the core theological declaration of this passage and leads to the trap of using modern physical knowledge to "falsify" the text—a reversal of priorities.

### Pitfall 2: "The absence of the phrase 'God saw that it was good' on the second day implies that the creation on that day was flawed."

**Clarification:**

The absence of the phrase does not equate to a judgment that it was "not good."

Nowhere in the entire book of Genesis is the firmament or the waters themselves declared to be evil. A more sound interpretation views this as a matter of literary structure: the order of the aquatic environment only reaches its full, closed-loop state on the third day, when dry land appears. Every realm of creation delineated by God is inherently part of His good creative design; there is no such thing as a "flawed" created entity (any dualistic line of reasoning must be firmly rejected).

### Pitfall 3: "The waters above and below represent spiritual forces of good and evil."

**Clarification:**

This is an instance of over-allegorizing—reading a post-Fall, dualistic conflict between good and evil into a creation narrative that precedes the Fall.

The second day belongs to the initial stage of creation, before the entry of sin. Both the waters above and the waters below are created entities, bounded and constrained by God; there is no inherent moral distinction—good or evil—within the waters themselves.

Allegorical interpretation must be grounded in the literal narrative; it cannot exist in a vacuum, detached from the narrative itself.

## V. Morning Meditation (Reflecting with God)

The second day reminds us that a stable environment for life is not a matter of chance or something to be taken for granted; rather, it is a gracious gift from God, who actively established boundaries and brought chaotic forces under His control. We often take breathing and beholding the vast sky for granted, failing to realize that these things are sustained by the sovereign hand of the Creator.

At the same time, this passage reminds us of the value of boundaries. Many view "boundaries" as constraints, longing to break through all limitations to act according to their own will; yet, the first chapter of Genesis reveals that the boundaries established by God are, in fact, the very prerequisites for safety, order, and blessing.

Without boundaries, the great waters would be nothing but chaos and destruction; it is only through the limits set by God that a space is created where life can dwell securely.

**Reflection Question:**

Do I perceive the boundaries God has set for my life—moral boundaries, the limits of human existence, or boundaries within relationships—as restrictive barriers I must break through? Or do I recognize them as expressions of His protection and grace?

## Summary and Preparation for the Next Section

Genesis 1:6–8 (the second day) completes the establishment of the first layer of spatial order. Through His word, God sets a "firmament" (expanse) amidst the vast, continuous waters, dividing the waters above from the waters below and defining the boundaries of these aquatic realms. By naming this expanse "heaven," God completes the second stage of the "establishment of domains" that spans the first three days—laying a narrative foundation for the creation of birds on the fifth day and foreshadowing the judgment of the Flood.

In the next section, we will examine Genesis 1:9–13 (the third day: the emergence of dry land and the creation of vegetation). This marks the conclusion of the "establishment of domains" phase, and it is here that we encounter, for the first time, a twofold declaration that "God saw that it was good"—now that both the aquatic and terrestrial orders have been fully established.
